## Overview

A buck converter steps a voltage down efficiently by switching the input on and off about 150,000 times a second
and smoothing the result, instead of burning the extra voltage as heat the way a linear regulator does. This one
turns a 12 V adapter into 5 V at up to 1 A.

It started in LTspice, moved to a schematic in Fusion Electronics, and was then built by hand from through-hole
parts on a 7 × 9 cm perfboard. A PCB version is the next step.

| Spec | Target | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input | 12 V adapter | 14.5 V measured with no load |
| Output | 5.00 V | 4.98 V no load, 4.97 V at 1 A |
| Load current | 1 A | 1 A (5 Ω, 25 W load resistor) |
| Regulator | LM2596T-ADJ | TO-220-5, 150 kHz switching |
| Build | Perfboard | 7 × 9 cm, wires on the solder side |

## How it works

While the switch inside U1 is on, current flows from the input through the inductor L1 into the output and charges
C_OUT. When the switch turns off, L1 keeps pushing current, and the Schottky diode D1 gives that current a path
around, so the output never sees a gap. The ratio of on-time to total time sets the output voltage, roughly
Vout = D × Vin, so 5 V from 12 V means the switch is on about 42% of the time. The regulator adjusts that duty
cycle on its own to hold the output steady.

I chose the LM2596T-ADJ because it holds the switch, oscillator, reference and error amplifier in one TO-220
package, handles 3 A, and needs only an inductor, a diode, two capacitors and a resistor divider around it. It is
also a through-hole part, which suits perfboard.

The pins mattered in practice:

1. **VIN:** the input supply.
2. **OUTPUT:** this is the switch node, not the regulated output. It feeds L1 and the cathode of D1.
3. **GND**
4. **FB:** feedback, which the chip regulates to 1.23 V.
5. **ON/OFF:** tied to ground to keep the chip enabled.

## Parts

| Ref | Part | Why this one |
| --- | ---- | ------------ |
| U1 | LM2596T-ADJ | Integrated 3 A switcher, through-hole TO-220, adjustable output |
| L1 | 68 µH toroid | The inductor sets the ripple current, so it must be rated above 1 A and not saturate. I had to search for the coiled through-hole type rather than a surface-mount one |
| D1 | 1N5822 Schottky | Low forward drop and fast recovery; a normal diode would waste more power and be too slow at 150 kHz |
| C_IN | 470 µF, 25 V or more | Bulk capacitor that supplies the input current pulses; the voltage rating needs margin over the 14.5 V seen at the input |
| C_BYP | 100 nF ceramic | Handles the fast, high-frequency spikes the large electrolytic is too slow for |
| C_OUT | 220 µF, 10 V or more | Smooths the output ripple on the 5 V rail |
| R1–R4 | 1% axial resistors | Set the output voltage (next section) |
| J_IN, J_OUT | 2-pin screw terminals | Connections for the adapter and the load |

The two input capacitors are in parallel on purpose: the big electrolytic stores charge but responds slowly, and the
small ceramic responds instantly to the switching edges.

## The feedback divider

The LM2596 adjusts its output until the voltage at the FB pin equals its 1.23 V reference. A resistor chain from
the output sets what fraction of the output the chip sees at FB:

- `Vout = Vref × (1 + Rtop / R1)`, with `Vref = 1.23 V`
- `Rtop = R1 × (Vout / Vref − 1) = 1000 × (5.00 / 1.23 − 1) ≈ 3065 Ω`

The first plan was 3 kΩ plus 68 Ω in series (3068 Ω), which gives 5.00 V. Those values weren't in my parts bin, so I
kept R1 at 1 kΩ and built the top half from series combinations of resistors I owned, comparing each prediction with
a measurement:

| Top chain | Total | Predicted | Measured |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 kΩ + 68 Ω (original plan, not on hand) | 3068 Ω | 5.00 V | not built |
| 2.2 kΩ + 680 Ω + 47 Ω | 2927 Ω | 4.83 V | 4.77 to 4.78 V |
| 2.2 kΩ + 680 Ω + 220 Ω (final) | 3100 Ω | 5.04 V | 4.98 V no load, 4.97 V at 1 A |

The final output is about 1.2% below the prediction. That is inside what to expect: the divider resistors are 1%,
and the LM2596's reference is specified over a range (about 1.19 V to 1.27 V), so a real chip rarely sits at exactly
1.23 V. The FB pin read 1.23 V on the finished board, which confirms the chip was regulating to the value in the
formula. The result that matters more: with the full 5 Ω load the output dropped only 0.01 V from no load.

## Problems and fixes

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fusion was slow and laggy | Cloud syncing of the design | Looked at alternatives, then stayed in Fusion |
| ERC said U1's GND pin was on a net named `n$2 1` | The ground wires were drawn as separate nets | Renamed the net to `GND` so every ground connects; ERC then showed zero errors |
| Screw terminals were a different type than planned | Received angled two-part terminals | Footprint marked as a placeholder; electrically identical |
| First reading was 5.4 V | A resistor in the chain was skipped in the wiring | Rewired the chain and re-measured |
| Readings of 5.2 V, then 4.77 V, then 4.78 V | A mix of wiring slips and a partial chain | Rebuilt the chain one resistor at a time, checking FB each time |
| A 2.2 kΩ resistor read 1.6 kΩ | I measured it while it was still in the circuit | In-circuit readings include parallel paths, so measure resistors out of the circuit |
| U1 got very hot at 1 A | A bare TO-220 dissipates about 1 to 1.5 W here | Unplugged it, planned a heatsink and a lighter test load |

I can't say for certain which single mistake caused each of the intermediate readings. Each wiring correction moved
the output closer to the prediction, the FB pin read 1.23 V to 1.27 V throughout (which fits the datasheet range),
and the final chain matched the calculation once it was wired exactly as designed.

## Heat and the move to a PCB

The perfboard version works, but U1 runs hot at full load, and that is the main thing a PCB should fix. At 1 A
with the input near 14.5 V, the LM2596 dissipates roughly 1 to 1.5 W in its switch and control circuitry, which is a
lot for a bare TO-220. The fixes are a heatsink on the tab, airflow, and a 10 Ω load (about 0.5 A) for longer runs.
A PCB can also spread heat into a copper pour under the tab.

**Status: the PCB version is in progress.** The next step is generating the board from the Fusion schematic and
laying out the switching loop: place U1, D1, L1 and the capacitors close together, route the high-current loop
first with wide traces, and add a ground pour with thermal relief for U1. The inductor and C_OUT footprints are
placeholders to check against the real parts before ordering. The perfboard measurements (4.98 V no load, 4.97 V
at 1 A) are the baseline the PCB should match or beat.

What I'm carrying forward:

- Measure resistors out of the circuit, and measure FB first when the output is wrong; it shows whether the divider
  or the chip is at fault.
- Name the ground net so every ground connects, and run ERC after every wiring change.
- Keep the switch-node loop (U1, D1, ground) short, and keep the FB wire short and away from L1, since L1 radiates
  noise.
- Check the real footprints of L1 and C_OUT before ordering.
