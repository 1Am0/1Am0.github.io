## Overview

A handwritten-digit classifier built from first principles: one `main.cpp`, the C++ standard library, and the
math worked out by hand. It reads the raw MNIST files, trains a 784 → 64 → 10 neural network with mini-batch
gradient descent and backpropagation, and classifies the 10,000 test digits it never saw during training.

This was the last step in a series of from-scratch projects: linear regression, a perceptron, logistic
regression, a [neural network that learns XOR](/projects/xor-neural-network/), and then this.

## How it works

1. **Loading the data:** MNIST is stored in the IDX binary format, a big-endian header followed by raw bytes.
   The loader assembles each 4-byte header field with shifts and ORs, validates the magic numbers, then reads
   the pixels in one bulk read and scales them to 0–1.
2. **Forward pass:** 64 sigmoid hidden neurons each take a weighted sum of all 784 pixels. Ten output neurons
   combine the hidden activations, and softmax turns their scores into probabilities (subtracting the largest
   score first so `exp` can't overflow).
3. **Loss:** cross-entropy, `-log(p[label])`. An untrained network scores about ln 10 ≈ 2.30.
4. **Backpropagation:** with softmax and cross-entropy, the output error is simply `p − one_hot(label)`; hidden
   errors are the weighted output errors times `h(1 − h)`.
5. **Training:** shuffle every epoch, walk through the data in mini-batches of 32, average the gradients, step.
6. **Checking the math:** the analytic gradients match a numerical finite-difference check to 8–10 significant
   digits for every parameter type.

| Setting | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Architecture | 784 → 64 sigmoid → 10 softmax |
| Parameters | 50,890 |
| Training | 60,000 images, batches of 32, 15 epochs, learning rate 0.1 |
| Test accuracy | **96.75%** |
| Training time | about 6 s per epoch on one CPU core |

## What the network learned

Each tile is one hidden neuron's 784 weights drawn as a 28×28 image: blue where ink turns the neuron on, red
where it turns it off. The neurons learned blurry stroke and curve detectors, not whole digits, and the output
layer combines them into a vote.

<Figure src={hiddenWeights} alt="The 64 hidden neurons' weights, each drawn as a 28 by 28 image of blue and red strokes" n={2} caption="Hidden layer / 64 learned weight maps" />

The most common mistakes are 4 → 9, 7 → 2, 5 → 3 and 9 → 4: pairs that differ by a single stroke.

## Experiments

**How many hidden neurons?** Same setup, 15 epochs, learning rate 0.5:

| Hidden neurons | 0 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 16 | 32 | 64 | 128 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Test accuracy (%) | 91.7 | 42.8 | 82.9 | 91.4 | 94.6 | 97.0 | 97.6 | 98.1 |

Two hidden neurons is worse than none, because squeezing every image through two numbers throws information
away. Past about 64, doubling the layer buys only about half a percent.

**Does a second hidden layer help?** Barely: 64 → 32 scored 97.68% against 97.61% for 64 alone, and a single
layer of 128 (98.13%) beat every two-layer version, since deeper sigmoid networks train slowly.

**Does position matter?** A lot. Every weight is tied to one fixed pixel, so shifting the test digits sideways
breaks the network quickly:

| Shift right | 0 px | 1 px | 2 px | 3 px | 4 px | 6 px |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Test accuracy (%) | 96.6 | 93.7 | 75.5 | 52.3 | 30.3 | 9.2 |

That's why convolutional networks exist, and why the draw-a-digit page centers drawings the way MNIST does.

## Interactive tools

Two companion web pages: a **network explorer** that follows a test digit through every neuron and shows the
training curves and confusion matrix, and a **draw-a-digit** page that converts your drawing into a
28×28 MNIST-style image (crop, scale to 20×20, center by mass) and runs it through the trained network.

## Next steps

ReLU instead of sigmoid, a validation split, ±2 px shifts as data augmentation, a convolutional layer, and
exporting the weights to run inference on an ESP32 microcontroller.

The network code was written by hand, step by step, with an AI assistant acting as a tutor for hints and code
review.
