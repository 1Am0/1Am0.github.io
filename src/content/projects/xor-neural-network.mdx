## The problem

Points are drawn uniformly from the square [−1, 1]². A point is labeled **1** when its two coordinates have
the same sign (top-right and bottom-left quadrants) and **0** otherwise: XOR spread over a continuous plane.

No single line can separate the two diagonal corners from the other two, so a linear model such as logistic
regression or a perceptron is stuck at about 50%, no better than guessing. A hidden layer fixes this: each
hidden neuron learns its own line, and the output neuron combines them into a bent boundary.

## The network

- **Hidden layer:** H sigmoid neurons, each computing `h = σ(w·x + b)`
- **Output:** one sigmoid neuron, `p = σ(v·h + c)`, the probability of class 1
- **Loss:** binary cross-entropy, with `p` clamped to avoid `log(0)`
- **Training:** full-batch gradient descent, learning rate 1.0, 10,000 epochs, on 800 samples (200 held out)
- **Initialization:** small random weights in ±0.5. All-zero weights would keep every hidden neuron identical
  forever, so random starts are needed to break the symmetry.

Backpropagation was derived by hand (output error `p − y`, hidden error `δo · v · h(1 − h)`) and checked
against a numerical central-difference gradient: all 9 parameters of the 2-neuron network matched to 7–8
significant digits.

## Results

| Model | Test accuracy | What it learns |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logistic regression / perceptron | ~50% | One straight line, no better than guessing |
| 2 hidden neurons | ~84% | A diagonal band between two parallel lines |
| 4 hidden neurons | ~97–99% | Four lines that combine into the quadrant pattern |
| 8 hidden neurons | ~98% | No more accurate than 4, but trains reliably on every seed |

Two neurons top out around 84% because, even if they learned the axes exactly, the output neuron would then
have to compute XOR of their answers: the same unsolvable problem, one layer up. The best it can do is a band.

With 4 neurons the network didn't find the "obvious" axis lines. It built a diamond of diagonal lines whose
combined vote lands on the axes. Neurons beyond about 4–6 mostly duplicate others or go flat; they don't add
accuracy, but they make it less likely that training gets stuck on an unlucky random start.

## Visualizing it

The program writes its predictions over a 60×60 grid, the test points and each hidden neuron's weights to a
CSV file, and a standalone viewer page plots them: the network's output across the square, misclassified
points, each hidden neuron's line, and a table of the learned weights.

## What I learned

- Why a hidden layer plus a nonlinearity is what lets a network go beyond straight-line boundaries
- Deriving and implementing backpropagation by hand, and verifying it numerically
- Why weights must start random, not zero
- How the number of hidden neurons trades accuracy against how reliably training works
